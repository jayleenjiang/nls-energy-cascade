\section{Introduction}

The transfer of energy between the modes of a nonlinear Hamiltonian system is a
basic question across the sciences, from fluids and oceanography to climate.
When external forcing and dissipation act on a few modes while the bulk---the
\emph{inertial range}---evolves intrinsically, the natural framework is
out-of-equilibrium statistical physics; for wave systems it is \emph{wave} (or
\emph{weak}) \emph{turbulence theory} \cite{Nazarenko}, in which energy is
transported between Fourier modes, that is, across spatial scales. The classical
description proceeds through the wave kinetic equation, whose power-law
stationary solutions are the Kolmogorov--Zakharov cascade spectra
$n(k)=|k|^{-d}$ and $n(k)=|k|^{-d+2/3}$ (the direct and inverse cascades). These
spectra are themselves nonequilibrium steady states (NESS), in which energy
flows at constant flux through the inertial range; their rigorous justification
remains largely open \cite{HLNS}.

Following \cite{HLNS}, we adopt a complementary viewpoint: rather than the
kinetic equation, we study the invariant measure---the NESS itself---of a
forced and dissipated reduction of the nonlinear Schr\"odinger (NLS) equation.
The present paper is the numerical companion to that program. We compute and
analyze the NESS of the energy cascade chain for the three-mode system treated
analytically in \cite{HLNS} and for long chains, where the analysis is open.

\subsection{Derivation of the model}\label{sec:derivation}

Consider the cubic NLS on a torus,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nls}
  -i\partial_t v + \Delta v = \pm |v|^2 v .
\end{equation}
In Fourier variables $a_K$ the cubic nonlinearity couples wavevectors with
$K_1-K_2+K_3=K$. Retaining only the \emph{resonant} interactions, those with
$|K_1|^2-|K_2|^2+|K_3|^2=|K|^2$, gives the resonant NLS system, which
approximates \eqref{eq:nls} for sufficiently small data. Colliander, Keel,
Staffilani, Takaoka and Tao \cite{CKSTT} restrict this system to a finite set of
frequencies
\[
  \Lambda=\Lambda_1\cup\Lambda_2\cup\cdots\cup\Lambda_n\subset\mathbb{Z}^2,
\]
on which the amplitude is constant within each generation $\Lambda_j$. The
generations are produced by an explicit combinatorial ``nuclear family''
construction that embeds successive $\Lambda_j$ in $\mathbb{Z}^2$ through exact
rational (Pythagorean) rotations, with $\Lambda_1$ supported at low and
$\Lambda_n$ at high frequency. Writing $c_j(t)$ for the common amplitude on
$\Lambda_j$, the resonant system collapses to the \emph{toy model} of
\cite{CKSTT},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toy}
  -i\dot c_j = 2\Big(\sum_k |c_k|^2\Big)c_j - |c_j|^2 c_j
  + 2\big(c_{j-1}^2 + c_{j+1}^2\big)\bar c_j,
  \qquad j=1,\dots,n,\quad c_0=c_{n+1}=0 .
\end{equation}
In \cite{CKSTT} this model is shown to admit an energy-cascade solution whose
mass, concentrated on $\Lambda_1$ at $t=0$, migrates to $\Lambda_n$ at a later
time---a deterministic route to the growth of Sobolev norms of \eqref{eq:nls}.

System \eqref{eq:toy} is Hamiltonian. In action--angle coordinates
$c_j=\sqrt{I_j}\,e^{i\varphi_j}$, with the \emph{bond angles}
$\theta_k:=2(\varphi_k-\varphi_{k-1})$, it is generated by the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hamiltonian}
  H(\bI,\bphi) \;=\; \Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n} I_k\Big)^{2}
  \;-\;\tfrac12\sum_{k=1}^{n} I_k^{2}
  \;+\; 2\sum_{k=2}^{n} I_{k-1} I_k \cos\theta_k ,
\end{equation}
with the Hamiltonian equations of motion
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ham-flow}
  -\partial_{\varphi_j}H = 4 I_j\big(I_{j-1}\sin\theta_j - I_{j+1}\sin\theta_{j+1}\big),
  \qquad
  \partial_{I_j}H = 2M - I_j + 2 I_{j-1}\cos\theta_j + 2 I_{j+1}\cos\theta_{j+1},
\end{equation}
where $M=\sum_k I_k$ and $I_0=I_{n+1}=0$.

\begin{remark}[Normalization convention]\label{rem:convention}
The Hamiltonian \eqref{eq:hamiltonian} is twice the action--angle Hamiltonian of
\cite{HLNS}; equivalently the drift \eqref{eq:ham-flow} is twice theirs, and the
equilibrium measure below carries the factor $1/2T$ rather than $1/T$. This
amounts to a rescaling of time and is fixed throughout so that every formula
matches the simulation code that produces our numerical data. For $n=3$ with the
middle mode as reference one writes $\theta_1=2(\varphi_1-\varphi_2)$,
$\theta_3=2(\varphi_3-\varphi_2)$, recovering the reduced coordinates of
\cite{HLNS}.
\end{remark}

To realize an energy cascade as a statistical steady state one injects energy at
the low-frequency end and extracts it at the high-frequency end: the two end
modes $j=1$ and $j=n$ are coupled to stochastic heat baths at temperatures $T_1$
and $T_n$. This produces the It\^o system
\begin{align}\label{eq:sde-bulk}
  \dd I_j   &= -\,\partial_{\varphi_j} H \dd t
             \;+\; b^{I}_j \dd t \;+\; \sigma^{I}_j \dd W^{I}_j, \\
  \dd \varphi_j &= \phantom{-}\partial_{I_j} H \dd t
             \;+\; b^{\varphi}_j \dd t \;+\; \sigma^{\varphi}_j \dd W^{\varphi}_j,
             \qquad j = 1,\dots,n, \nonumber
\end{align}
in which the bath drift and diffusion $(b,\sigma)$ are supported only on
$j\in\{1,n\}$. We take the fluctuation--dissipation (Langevin) form: at the hot
end $j=1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bath}
  b^{I}_1 = 2\gamma\big(2T_1 - I_1\,\partial_{I_1}H\big),\quad
  \sigma^{I}_1 = 2\sqrt{2\gamma T_1 I_1},\quad
  b^{\varphi}_1 = -2\gamma I_2 \sin\theta_2,\quad
  \sigma^{\varphi}_1 = \sqrt{2\gamma T_1/I_1},
\end{equation}
and at the cold end
\[
  b^{I}_n=2\gamma\big(2T_n-I_n\,\partial_{I_n}H\big),\qquad
  b^{\varphi}_n=2\gamma I_{n-1}\sin\theta_n,
\]
with the corresponding replacements $T_1\to T_n$ in the diffusion
coefficients. Here $\gamma>0$ is the coupling strength. The bath
\eqref{eq:bath} is constructed so that at equilibrium
$T_1=T_n=T$ the system admits the explicit Gibbs invariant density
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gibbs}
  \rho_{\mathrm{eq}}(\bI,\bphi) \;\propto\; \exp\!\big(-H(\bI,\bphi)/2T\big).
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium consistency of the bath]\label{prop:gibbs-bath}
Assume $T_1=T_n=T$ and impose the natural no-flux boundary condition at
$I_j=0$. For every smooth observable $f$ that is periodic in the angles and
compactly supported in the action variables,
\[
  \int \Lgen f(\bI,\bphi)\,\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}(\bI,\bphi)\,\dd\bI\,\dd\bphi=0,
\]
where $\Lgen$ is the generator of \eqref{eq:sde-bulk}--\eqref{eq:bath}. Hence
\eqref{eq:gibbs} is a stationary density for the equal-temperature process,
provided the process is nonexplosive.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Split $\Lgen$ into the Hamiltonian transport and the two endpoint bath
operators. The Hamiltonian vector field is divergence-free in
$(\bI,\bphi)$ and conserves $H$, so its adjoint annihilates every density that
is a function of $H$. At each endpoint, the drift and diffusion coefficients in
\eqref{eq:bath} are in fluctuation--dissipation balance with inverse
temperature $1/(2T)$; a direct integration-by-parts calculation, with the
no-flux condition at $I_j=0$, shows that the endpoint adjoint also annihilates
$\exp(-H/2T)$. When $T_1=T_n=T$, the two endpoint calculations use the same
temperature, and summing the adjoint contributions gives
$\Ladj\rho_{\mathrm{eq}}=0$ in the weak sense above.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Choice of thermostat]\label{rem:bath}
The analysis of \cite{HLNS} uses a different thermostat---dissipation of the form
$\gamma(T_j-I_j^3)$ with a tailored multiplicative angle noise---chosen to make
the Lyapunov construction tractable; under that choice the Gibbs measure
\eqref{eq:gibbs} is \emph{not} invariant, even at equal temperatures. For our
numerical study we instead use the fluctuation--dissipation bath
\eqref{eq:bath}, whose equilibrium measure is the explicit Gibbs density
\eqref{eq:gibbs}; this provides a stringent check on the Fokker--Planck solver
(\cref{sec:fp-numerics}). The reduced-system analysis of the stabilization
mechanism in \cref{sec:stabilization} retains the original $\gamma(T-I^3)$ bath
of \cite{HLNS} for direct comparison with the proof.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Known results and open problems}\label{sec:known}

For the three-mode chain ($n=3$), in which only the middle mode $I_2$ is
unforced, Hani, Li, Nahmod and Staffilani \cite{HLNS} establish the existence,
uniqueness and ergodicity of the NESS: when the two bath temperatures are
sufficiently different, the system possesses a unique invariant probability
measure $\pi$, and the law of the process converges to $\pi$ in total variation
at a power-law rate in time. The proof rests on a novel construction of a
Lyapunov function through an elliptic Feynman--Kac equation. The central
difficulty is that, unlike microscopic heat conduction where energy simply flows
from hot to cold, here the transfer of energy to and from the unforced middle
mode depends sensitively on the phase variables being ``correct'': the system
must be prevented both from \emph{overheating} (energy trapped in $I_2$) and from
\emph{freezing} (energy blocked at $I_2$), and which of these threatens depends on
whether $I_2$ is large or small. This phase-dependent stabilization mechanism is
the analytical heart of \cite{HLNS} and the object of our \cref{sec:stabilization}.

Two limitations of the analytical theory motivate the present work. First, the
results of \cite{HLNS} are confined to $n=3$; the authors explicitly leave longer
chains open, noting that already the three-mode case is demanding. Second, the
NESS is not given in closed form, so quantitative properties---action profiles,
transport coefficients, local statistics, the relaxation spectrum---are
inaccessible to the existence theory. Both gaps are naturally addressed
numerically.

\subsection{Positioning within nonequilibrium transport}
\label{sec:positioning}

The present problem sits at the intersection of two related but distinct
literatures. In wave turbulence, one studies nonequilibrium cascades generated
by resonant wave interactions and their kinetic stationary spectra
\cite{Nazarenko}. The cascade chain used here is instead a finite resonant
subsystem of cubic NLS, inherited from the CKSTT construction \cite{CKSTT} and
from the three-mode NESS theory of \cite{HLNS}; the chain index is a generation
label in frequency space rather than a physical lattice coordinate. In
low-dimensional nonequilibrium statistical mechanics, anomalous transport is
often studied through oscillator chains with local conservation laws and
boundary reservoirs \cite{LepriLiviPoliti,Dhar2008,Spohn2014}. Our transport
observable is the conserved action current of the resonant model, not the
physical heat current of an anharmonic chain. Accordingly, any measured
finite-size exponent must be interpreted as a numerical law for this model,
not as a universal conductivity exponent.

This distinction also limits the local-equilibrium and fluctuation claims.
Local equilibrium is a standard organizing principle in hydrodynamic
descriptions of one-dimensional transport \cite{Spohn2014}, but the Hamiltonian
\eqref{eq:hamiltonian} contains a nonlocal mass term, so even equilibrium
two-site marginals are not strict Gibbs marginals of an isolated bond. We
therefore test a weaker pair-marginal rescaling rather than claim local Gibbs
convergence. Likewise, fluctuation relations for deterministic and stochastic
nonequilibrium steady states \cite{GallavottiCohen,LebowitzSpohn1999} motivate
future entropy-production measurements, but finite-time action-current averages
alone do not constitute such a test. A Gallavotti--Cohen analysis would require
a separate boundary-energy accumulator and is outside the claims made here.

\subsection{Contributions and organization}\label{sec:contributions}

This paper is a numerical study of the NESS of the energy cascade model
\eqref{eq:sde-bulk}. After collecting the thermodynamic and spectral framework in
\cref{sec:thermo,sec:fp,sec:methods}, we present two groups of results.

The division between the two groups is deliberate. The long-chain experiments
probe macroscopic statistical structure in a regime where no invariant-measure
theory is currently available, whereas the short-chain computations provide a
low-dimensional view of the stabilization and phase-locking mechanisms behind
the rigorously understood three-mode NESS.

\Cref{sec:long-chain} treats the \emph{long chain}, the regime left open by
\cite{HLNS}. We characterize the nonequilibrium steady state through its action
profile and an approximate local equilibrium in which adjacent-mode pair
marginals match a rescaled equilibrium marginal at the local kinetic
temperature, without asserting strict local-Gibbs structure. We also formulate
the transport problem for the conserved action current, keeping it distinct
from Hamiltonian-energy exchange with the baths.

\Cref{sec:short-chain} returns to the \emph{three-mode chain} for a detailed
study. We solve the stationary Fokker--Planck equation by a mesh-free,
data-driven neural-network method \cite{ZhaiDobsonLi}, validating it against the
exact Gibbs measure \eqref{eq:gibbs} in equilibrium; we then examine the
nonequilibrium steady state, the phase-locking stabilization mechanism, and the
observable-dependent slow relaxation visible in trajectory data. The numerical
tools---a high-throughput SIMD Monte Carlo integrator, a kernel density estimator
for the reference measure, and a trajectory-based backward solver for slow-mode
diagnostics---are described as they arise.


\section{Preliminary}

This section collects the thermodynamic observables we measure for the long
chain (\cref{sec:thermo}), the spectral (Fokker--Planck) framework for the
short chain (\cref{sec:fp}), and the numerical methods used to solve the
stationary Fokker--Planck equation and to extract eigenfunctions of the
generator (\cref{sec:methods}), and the validation protocol for the long-chain
Monte Carlo calculations (\cref{sec:mc-validation}).

\subsection{Nonequilibrium thermodynamics of the chain}\label{sec:thermo}

The Hamiltonian flow \eqref{eq:ham-flow} conserves the total mass
$M=\sum_k I_k$, and the evolution of each action obeys a discrete continuity
equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuity}
  \dot I_j \;=\; J_j - J_{j+1},
  \qquad
  J_j \;:=\; 4\, I_{j-1} I_j \sin\theta_j ,
\end{equation}
so that $J_j$ is the \emph{action current} flowing from mode $j-1$ into mode
$j$ (with $J_1=J_{n+1}=0$ for the closed chain). In the nonequilibrium steady
state (NESS) the expected actions are stationary, $\E[\dot I_j]=0$, hence the
mean current is uniform along the chain, $\E[J_j]\equiv \E[J(n)]$ for all interior
$j$.

\begin{definition}[Action conductivity and Fourier's-law analogy]\label{de:fourier}
Assume $T_1>T_n$, so the positive action-current direction points from the hot
left bath toward the cold right bath. Let
\[
  \nabla T(n):=\frac{T_n-T_1}{n}<0
\]
denote the effective temperature gradient in that positive spatial direction.
We say, by analogy with Fourier's law, that the chain has finite action
conductivity $\kappa$ if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourier}
  \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty}\frac{\E[J(n)]}{\nabla T(n)} \;=\; -
  \kappa \in (0,\infty).
\end{equation}

Let $\kappa_n=-\E[J(n)]/\nabla T(n)$ be the finite-chain action
conductivity. Anomalous transport corresponds to $\kappa_n\sim n^{\alpha}$
with $\alpha\neq0$:
$\alpha>0$ is superdiffusive and $\alpha<0$ (so $\kappa_n\to0$) signals an
insulator.
\end{definition}

When one measures the actual Hamiltonian energy exchanged with the baths, two
further observables characterize the NESS. The \emph{entropy production rate}
associated with the two heat baths is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:entropy-prod}
  \dot S \;=\; -\frac{\E[\dot Q_1]}{T_1} - \frac{\E[\dot Q_n]}{T_n} \;\ge\; 0,
\end{equation}
where $\dot Q_1,\dot Q_n$ are the mean heat fluxes delivered by the baths; in
the NESS the energy balance forces $\E[\dot Q_1]=-\E[\dot Q_n]=:\E[\dot Q]$ and
$\dot S = \E[\dot Q]\,(1/T_n - 1/T_1)$. The second law $\dot S\ge 0$ is refined
by the steady-state \emph{fluctuation theorem} of Gallavotti--Cohen
\cite{GallavottiCohen}: under its usual chaoticity and large-deviation
hypotheses, writing $\Sigma_\tau$ for the entropy produced over a window of
length $\tau$, the corresponding densities satisfy formally
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ft}
  \lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\,
  \ln\frac{p_\tau(a)}{p_\tau(-a)} \;=\; a .
\end{equation}
In this paper the transport measurements below concern the conserved
\emph{action} current. A full bath-energy and entropy-production measurement is
left as a separate numerical diagnostic and is not used as evidence for the main claims.

\subsection{Fokker--Planck equation and spectral decomposition}\label{sec:fp}

Write $x=(\bI,\bphi)$ and let the system \eqref{eq:sde-bulk} have drift $f(x)$
and diffusion matrix $\Sigma(x)=\sigma(x)\sigma(x)^{\!\top}$. The probability
density $u(x,t)$ evolves by the (forward) Fokker--Planck, or Kolmogorov forward,
equation $\partial_t u = \Ladj u$, where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fp-operator}
  \Ladj u = -\sum_i \partial_{x_i}\!\big(f_i\,u\big)
            + \tfrac12\sum_{i} \partial^{2}_{x_i}\!\big(\Sigma_{ii}\,u\big).
\end{equation}
For our model the diffusion is diagonal and degenerate: only the bath modes
carry noise, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:diffusion}
  \tfrac12\Sigma_{I_j I_j} = 4\gamma T_j I_j, \qquad
  \tfrac12\Sigma_{\varphi_j\varphi_j} = \gamma T_j/I_j,
  \qquad j\in\{1,n\},
\end{equation}
and zero otherwise in the full $(\bI,\bphi)$ coordinates. In reduced
coordinates built from doubled bond angles, for instance
$\theta_2=2(\varphi_2-\varphi_1)$ at the left boundary, the angular diffusion
coefficient is multiplied by $2^2$, giving
$\tfrac12\Sigma_{\theta_2\theta_2}=4\gamma T_1/I_1$ (and similarly at the right
boundary).

\begin{remark}[Degeneracy / hypoellipticity]\label{rem:hypo}
Because noise enters only at $j\in\{1,n\}$, $\Sigma$ has rank $4$ in the
$2n$-dimensional state space; the interior modes are forced only through the
Hamiltonian coupling. Existence, uniqueness and ergodicity of the invariant
measure for the $n=3$ system are established in \cite{HLNS} via a
Feynman--Kac--Lyapunov argument together with H\"ormander's condition; the long
chain $n>3$ is left there as an open problem and is the subject of
\cref{sec:long-chain}.
\end{remark}

The stationary density solves $\Ladj u = 0$ with $\int u\dd x = 1$. The
long-time relaxation toward it is governed by the (backward) generator $\Lgen$,
the formal adjoint of $\Ladj$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generator}
  \Lgen g = \sum_i f_i\,\partial_{x_i} g
          + \tfrac12\sum_i \Sigma_{ii}\,\partial^2_{x_i} g,
  \qquad
  (\E_x[g(X_t)]) = (e^{t\Lgen} g)(x).
\end{equation}
Under ergodicity the spectrum of $\Lgen$ lies in $\{\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda\le 0\}$
with a simple eigenvalue $\lambda_0=0$ (constant eigenfunction). Ordering the
remaining eigenvalues by real part, the \emph{spectral gap}
$\lambda_R := \max\{\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda : \lambda\neq 0\}<0$ sets the slowest
relaxation rate, and for an observable $g$ orthogonal to constants
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decay}
  \E_x[g(X_t)] - \E_{\mathrm{eq}}[g]
  \;\sim\; Q(x)\,e^{\lambda_R t}\quad (t\to\infty),
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is the corresponding (sub)dominant eigenfunction, $\Lgen Q =
\lambda_R Q$. A purely real $\lambda_R$ indicates an overdamped relaxation; a
nonzero imaginary part $\lambda_I$ indicates damped oscillation with frequency
$\lambda_I$.

\subsection{Numerical methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsubsection{A data-driven neural-network Fokker--Planck solver}
Classical grid-based PDE solvers are impractical for \eqref{eq:fp-operator} in
dimension $2n$: the domain is unbounded with no natural boundary condition, and
the cost grows exponentially with dimension. We follow the mesh-free,
data-driven solver of Zhai--Dobson--Li \cite{ZhaiDobsonLi} (building on
\cite{Li2019,DobsonLiZhai}), which represents the stationary density by a neural
network $\tilde u(x;\vartheta)$ trained against the unconstrained loss
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nn-loss}
  \mathcal{J}(\vartheta)
  = \underbrace{\frac{1}{N_X}\sum_{i}\big(\Ladj \tilde u(x_i;\vartheta)\big)^2}_{\text{PDE residual } L_1}
  + \underbrace{\frac{1}{N_Y}\sum_{j}\big(\tilde u(y_j;\vartheta)-v(y_j)\big)^2}_{\text{MC reference } L_2},
\end{equation}
where $\Ladj\tilde u$ is evaluated by automatic differentiation and $v$ is a
low-accuracy Monte Carlo (MC) estimate of the density at reference points
$\{y_j\}$. The operator term $L_1$ regularizes the network toward solutions of
$\Ladj u=0$ and sharply reduces the amount and accuracy of MC data required;
the method tolerates spatially uncorrelated noise in $v$ \cite{ZhaiDobsonLi}.

We adapt the solver to \eqref{eq:sde-bulk} in two ways. First, the angles are
periodic, so the network input is the \emph{periodic encoding}
$(\bI,\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1,\dots)$ rather than the raw angles. Second, the
diffusion \eqref{eq:diffusion} is degenerate (\cref{rem:hypo}); we therefore
include collocation points in the zero-density region and constrain them by the
operator $L_1$ alone (no fabricated reference data there). The reference density
$v$ is obtained by a high-throughput MC simulation of \eqref{eq:sde-bulk}
together with a kernel density estimate; see \cref{sec:fp-numerics}.

\subsubsection{Eigenfunctions of the generator}
To probe \eqref{eq:decay} we estimate the relaxation rate and slow-mode
amplitude visible to a chosen observable directly from trajectories. For a
localized observable $g$ (we use
$g=\cos\theta_1$) we evolve a large ensemble of trajectories from each of many
initial points $x$ and record the conditional expectation $\E_x[g(X_t)]$ as a
function of $t$. Fitting a late-time exponential window gives an
observable-dependent estimate of the dominant decay rate visible to $g$; if a
damped oscillatory fit is also stable, it gives an estimate of an imaginary
part. The fitted amplitude gives a pointwise sample $Q(x)$ of the associated
slow mode.
A second network is then trained to represent $Q$ on the whole domain, using a
data loss against the sampled $Q(x)$ together with a PDE loss enforcing the
eigenvalue equation $\Lgen Q=\lambda_R Q$. These fits are diagnostics rather
than proof that the estimated rate is the window-independent spectral gap of
the full generator. Results are reported in
\cref{sec:eigenfunction}.

\subsection{Monte Carlo validation and uncertainty protocol}\label{sec:mc-validation}

The long-chain histogram and reference-density calculations use the SIMD
Euler--Maruyama workflow, while the burn-in and finite-time current studies use
a separate double-precision canonical accumulator. The two
data streams are analyzed separately and are not pooled. Current uncertainties
are computed across independent trajectory averages; equal subwindows of a
trajectory are used only to diagnose finite-time drift, not as independent
replicates.

Before a production dataset is used as evidence, the corresponding executable
is checked for seeded reproducibility, agreement across thread counts,
equal-temperature zero current, current reversal after swapping the baths, and
sensitivity to timestep and burn-in. Scaling fits, when reported, must state
the fitted chain-length window and attach uncertainty intervals obtained by
resampling independent trajectories. These criteria define the acceptance
protocol; quantitative outcomes are reported with the experiment to which they
apply.

\section{Statistical physics properties of the long cascade chain}\label{sec:long-chain}

The rigorous analysis of \cite{HLNS} treats the three-mode chain and lists the
longer chains $n>3$ as open. This section studies those long chains
numerically under the Gibbs-preserving two-bath dynamics \eqref{eq:bath}: the
structure of the nonequilibrium steady state (\cref{sec:ness-long}), the
transport of the conserved action current and the associated finite-chain
conductivity (\cref{sec:thermal-conductivity}), and finite-time current
fluctuations. We keep the terminology ``action current'' throughout, because
\eqref{eq:continuity} is a continuity equation for the conserved action
$M=\sum_j I_j$; Hamiltonian energy exchange with the baths is a distinct
observable.

\paragraph{Simulation.}
All long-chain data are produced by a single Euler--Maruyama integrator of
\eqref{eq:sde-bulk} that processes $W=16$ independent trajectories in lockstep
(one SIMD register per arithmetic operation), with the phases wrapped to
$[-\pi,\pi)$ at every step. Unless otherwise stated we use $\gamma=0.1$ and the
closed two-bath chain \eqref{eq:bath}. The local-equilibrium histograms use
long on-the-fly accumulation runs, while the current-scaling runs use an
independent canonical current accumulator with double precision, deterministic
trajectory seeds, four equal current blocks per trajectory, and explicit
timestep and burn-in diagnostics.

\subsection{Nonequilibrium steady state}\label{sec:ness-long}

\paragraph{Stabilization.}
We regard a run as stabilized when the sampled expectations (in particular the
action profile $\langle I_j\rangle$ and the bond-angle statistics) cease to
change with increasing sample size. Because the cascade transports energy
slowly, the relaxation time is long and the burn-in must be chosen with care:
a short run can report a spuriously uniform profile that has not yet developed
the steady gradient. We return to this point below.

\paragraph{Action profile.}
\Cref{fig:profile} shows the mean action profiles $\langle I_j\rangle$ for
closed chains with $T_1=10$, $T_n=2$ at $n=25,50,100$. The profiles are
monotone after a short boundary layer at the hot contact and show the sharpest
drop at the cold bath. For $n=25$, the profile decreases from
$\langle I_1\rangle=0.738$ at the hot end to $\langle I_{13}\rangle=0.573$ near
mid-chain and $\langle I_{25}\rangle=0.165$ at the cold contact. The
corresponding endpoint values are $(0.514,0.106)$ at $n=50$ and
$(0.362,0.073)$ at $n=100$. These profile estimates are the data used below to
define the local kinetic temperature in the LTE comparison.

\paragraph{Cascade geometry.}
The interpretation of the chain as an energy cascade rests on the frequency
embedding of \cite{CKSTT}: each generation of the resonant ``nuclear family''
construction places a group of modes on $\mathbb{Z}^2$ by exact rational
(Pythagorean) rotations, so that the abstract chain position $j$ corresponds to
a concrete set of frequencies $\Lambda_j$. \Cref{fig:embedding} plots the
locations of the first five generations used in the CKSTT construction. The
open-chain terminal-energy scaling is not used as evidence in the present
paper; establishing it would require a separate convergence study under the
same corrected thermostat used for the closed-chain transport measurements.

\paragraph{Local thermodynamic equilibrium.}\label{par:lte}
A central question is whether, despite the global nonequilibrium drive,
adjacent modes in the bulk are described \emph{locally} by an equilibrium
measure. Let $q(I_a,I_{a+1},\theta_a)$ denote the joint pair density in the
NESS, with $(I_a,I_b)=(I_j,I_{j+1})$, and let
$p_T(I_a,I_{a+1},\theta_a)$ be the corresponding marginal of the equilibrium
measure at temperature $T$, whose full density is proportional to
$\exp[-H/(2T)]$ under the convention of \cref{rem:convention} (integrating out
all other modes). We accumulate $q$ and $p_T$ on
$80^3$ histograms over $[0,4]^2\times[-\pi,\pi)$ from $\sim10^9$ samples per
site, at interior pairs of the closed chain ($T_1=10,T_n=2$) for
$n=25,50,100$.

First, neither $q$ nor $p_T$ equals the two-mode Gibbs density
$\exp\!\big[-(\tfrac12(I_a{+}I_b)^2-\tfrac14(I_a^2{+}I_b^2)+I_aI_b\cos\theta)\big]$:
comparing $-\log(\text{PDF})$ to this form, the NESS and the equilibrium
marginal deviate in the \emph{same} way (both rise along the axes where the
two-mode form is flat, and their $\theta$-modulation is weaker). Since the
equilibrium marginal already deviates identically, the discrepancy comes from
marginalizing the nonlocal term $\tfrac12(\sum_j I_j)^2$, not from the
nonequilibrium drive: there is no strict local Gibbs structure, even at
equilibrium.

Second, the NESS marginal is nonetheless well approximated by a
\emph{rescaled} equilibrium marginal. We fit $\log q = x\log p_6 + c$ by
weighted least squares, weighting each bin by its count (the inverse variance
of the log-density for multinomial counts); statistical errors on $x$ are
below $3\times10^{-4}$ and are negligible against the systematic spread
discussed below. Restricted to the core ($I_a,I_b<2.5$) the fit is excellent,
with weighted $R^2$ from $0.986$ to $0.999$ (\cref{tab:lte}), and the rescaled
$p_6^{\,x}$ matches $q$ closely (\cref{fig:lte-resid}).

We caution against reading the slope as a temperature through the naive
relation $6/x$. A control fit between two simulated equilibria,
$\log p_{7.12}=x\log p_6+c$, returns $x=0.926$ rather than the naive
$6/7.12=0.843$: the marginals do not scale as $p_T=p_6^{\,6/T}$,
because the mean field $\langle\sum_j I_j\rangle\propto\sqrt{nT}$ shifts with
temperature. Hence $6/x$ systematically underestimates the effective
temperature.

Third, the effective temperature is the local kinetic temperature. Define
\[
  T_{\mathrm{kin}}(a)=
  6\,\frac{\langle I_a\rangle_{\mathrm{NESS}}}
          {\langle I_a\rangle_{\mathrm{eq}}}
\]
directly from the action profiles, independent of the marginal fit. Matching
$q$ to an actual simulated equilibrium confirms
this scale: at $a/n=0.48$ the NESS marginal fitted against the true $p_{7.12}$
gives $x=1.01$, i.e.\ $q\approx p_{7.12}$, within a few percent of
$T_{\mathrm{kin}}=6.85$ at that site (and consistent to $0.2\%$ with the
$q$--$p_6$ and $p_{7.12}$--$p_6$ fits combined). The kinetic temperature, the
equilibrium-matching temperature, and the naive $6/x$ agree to within
$5$--$10\%$, the spread reflecting the residual departure of $q$ from a true
equilibrium marginal. To leading order, then, the dependence of the effective
temperature on $(T_L,T_R,a/n)$ is simply the shape of the temperature profile;
across boundary pairs of equal mean ($(8,4),(9,3),(10,2)$) it varies
monotonically with the imposed gradient.

Fourth, the approximation improves as the chain lengthens. The control fit
between two equilibria ($p_{7.12}$ vs.\ $p_6$) gives a weighted $R^2=0.9997$,

\section{Detailed property of the short chain}\label{sec:short-chain}

We now turn to the smallest nontrivial cascade, the three-mode system $n=3$,
for which existence, uniqueness and ergodicity of the NESS are rigorously known
\cite{HLNS}. The Hamiltonian and bath of \cref{sec:long-chain} depend on the
phases only through $\theta_1=2(\varphi_1-\varphi_2)$ and
$\theta_3=2(\varphi_3-\varphi_2)$, so the dynamics reduces from the six
variables $(\bI,\bphi)$ to the five-dimensional state
$x=(I_1,I_2,I_3,\theta_1,\theta_3)$, in which the middle mode $I_2$ carries no
bath. This low dimension lets us solve the stationary Fokker--Planck equation
directly and interrogate the invariant measure (\cref{sec:fp-numerics}), the
mechanism that stabilizes the NESS (\cref{sec:stabilization}), and the spectrum
of the generator (\cref{sec:eigenfunction}).

\subsection{Numerical solution of the nonequilibrium steady state}\label{sec:fp-numerics}

In the reduced coordinates the stationary density solves $\Ladj u=0$, with
$\Ladj$ as in \eqref{eq:fp-operator}: the drift is the $n=3$ specialization of
\eqref{eq:sde-bulk}, and the (degenerate) diffusion \eqref{eq:diffusion} acts
only on $(I_1,\theta_1)$ and $(I_3,\theta_3)$ with coefficients
$4\gamma T_1 I_1,\ 4\gamma T_1/I_1$ and $4\gamma T_3 I_3,\ 4\gamma T_3/I_3$. We
solve it with the neural-network method of \cref{sec:methods}. The reference
density $v$ is produced by a long SIMD Monte Carlo integration of
\eqref{eq:sde-bulk} followed by a kernel density estimate on a grid of boxes in
$x$; boxes in which the Monte Carlo records no sample are retained as
zero-density collocation points and are constrained by the operator term $L_1$
alone. The network outputs the log-density, $u(x)$ with $\rho=\exp(\sigma_0
u+\mu_0)$ for fixed normalization constants $(\mu_0,\sigma_0)$, takes the
periodic encoding $(I_1,I_2,I_3,\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1,\cos\theta_3,
\sin\theta_3)$ as input, and is trained in two phases: a data-only pre-training
against $\{v(y_j)\}$, followed by joint training in which the weight of the PDE
residual $L_1$ is ramped on gradually.

\paragraph{Validation against the equilibrium Gibbs measure.}
When $T_1=T_3=T$ the exact invariant density is the Gibbs measure
\eqref{eq:gibbs}, which provides a stringent test. Training at $T=5$ and
comparing the network density to \eqref{eq:gibbs} on the slices $I_1=I_2=I_3=I$
(\cref{fig:eq-validation}), the mean relative error is $1.5\%$, $2.6\%$ and
$6.4\%$ at $I=0.5,1,2$ respectively, with the pointwise $\log$-ratio
$\log(\rho_{\mathrm{NN}}/\rho_{\mathrm{Gibbs}})$ confined to a narrow band. The
solver thus recovers the known equilibrium measure across the range of actions
that carry appreciable probability.

With distinct bath temperatures $T_1=2$, $T_3=8$ the invariant measure is no
longer of Gibbs form. \Cref{fig:neq-density} shows the network density on the
angular plane $(\theta_1,\theta_3)$ at several values of the common action: the
distribution concentrates around a phase-locked configuration and is visibly
distorted relative to the equilibrium measure. The departure from equilibrium
is quantified in \cref{sec:stabilization}.

\subsection{Mechanism of stabilization}\label{sec:stabilization}

The NESS is stabilized by a phase-locking mechanism that operates differently in
the high- and low-energy regimes \cite{HLNS}. At high energy the bond angles are
driven to a stable equilibrium near $2\pi/3$, escaping the unstable
configuration at $4\pi/3$; at low energy the angle $\theta_3$ remains near
$4\pi/3$, which prevents the middle action $I_2$ from collapsing to zero and
keeps the cascade alive. The reduced four-dimensional subsystem
$(I_1,I_3,\theta_1,\theta_3)$, obtained in the low-energy limit and equipped
with the $\gamma(T-I^3)$ thermostat of \cite{HLNS}, isolates this mechanism; we
solve its stationary Fokker--Planck equation by the same network method and
recover the predicted concentration of $\theta_3$.

We retain two source-traced diagnostics of the nonequilibrium drive at
$T_1=2,T_3=8$: symmetry breaking in the angular density and the phase-locking
branch predicted by the reduced equation.

\emph{(i) Symmetry breaking.} At equilibrium the density is symmetric under
$\theta_1\leftrightarrow\theta_3$. Out of equilibrium this symmetry is broken:
the relative asymmetry
$|\rho(\theta_1,\theta_3)-\rho(\theta_3,\theta_1)|/
(\rho(\theta_1,\theta_3)+\rho(\theta_3,\theta_1))$ is visibly nonzero on the
high-density part of the angular plane (\cref{fig:symmetry}). We report the
qualitative effect here and reserve the precise max/mean asymmetry for the
source-traced grid recomputation, since it depends sensitively on the density
mask and normalization.

\emph{(ii) Phase-locking angle.} The most probable bond angle is shifted from
its equilibrium value to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phase-lock}
  \theta_0 \;=\; \pi-\arcsin\!\frac{\sqrt{4\gamma^2+3}-\gamma}{2(\gamma^2+1)},
\end{equation}
the stable branch that tends to $2\pi/3$ as $\gamma\to0$. We use the density
plots qualitatively to display concentration near the stable sector; no
quantitative peak-location table is used as an independent result here.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:eq47}
Equivalently, writing $s=\sin\theta_0$, the reduced angular fixed-point
condition gives the quadratic
\[
  4(1+\gamma^2)s^2+4\gamma s-3=0 .
\]
The positive root is
\[
  s=\frac{\sqrt{4\gamma^2+3}-\gamma}{2(1+\gamma^2)} .
\]
The inverse sine has two branches. The branch in \eqref{eq:phase-lock} lies in
$(\pi/2,\pi)$ and tends to $2\pi/3$ as $\gamma\to0$, matching the stable
high-energy phase-locking equilibrium; the complementary branch tends to
$\pi/3$ and is not the observed stable lock.
\end{remark}

Finally, current conservation through the unforced middle mode gives the
balance condition
$\E[\,I_2 I_1\sin\theta_1\,]+\E[\,I_2 I_3\sin\theta_3\,]\approx0$ in a
stationary state. We use this identity as a rerun diagnostic for the solver,
but do not report it as a separate quantitative result here.

\subsection{Eigenfunction of the generator}\label{sec:eigenfunction}

To probe the relaxation spectrum we apply the trajectory method of
\cref{sec:methods} with the localized observable $g=\cos\theta_1$. From each of
$4096$ initial points we evolve an ensemble and record
$\E_x[\cos\theta_1(X_t)]$; fitting the decay \eqref{eq:decay} returns, at each
initial point, an estimate of the observable-dependent relaxation rate and
amplitude.

At $\gamma=0.1$, pure-exponential fits
\[
  \E_x[\cos\theta_1(X_t)] \approx A(x)e^{\lambda t}+c(x)
\]
over the full recorded window $t\in[0,5]$ retain $2895$ of $4096$ curves under
the filter $-5<\lambda<0$, with median
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda_diag}
  \lambda_{\mathrm{diag}} \;\approx\; -0.934 .
\end{equation}
This number is reproducible from the saved backward-Monte-Carlo curves, but it
is not a window-independent spectral gap estimate: the retained median shifts
from $-1.60$ on $t\in[0,2]$ to $-0.68$ on $t\in[0.5,5]$
(\cref{fig:eigenvalues}). We therefore use $\lambda_{\mathrm{diag}}$ as a
diagnostic relaxation rate for the observable $\cos\theta_1$, not as a resolved
statement about the full generator spectrum.

The fitted amplitudes provide pointwise samples $Q(x)$ of the corresponding
slow-mode surrogate. A second network is trained to represent $Q$ subject to
the eigenvalue equation $\Lgen Q=\lambda_{\mathrm{diag}} Q$, using the generator
$\Lgen$ of \eqref{eq:generator} (without the divergence terms that distinguish
$\Ladj$). The reconstruction is only moderately accurate: the data
root-mean-square error is $0.38$ (relative error $0.71$) and the median
normalized PDE residual $|\Lgen Q-\lambda_{\mathrm{diag}} Q|/|Q|$ is $0.35$. We
report these candidly: the fitted amplitude data contain both signs and are
reconstructed from intrinsically noisy backward Monte Carlo data, making this
the hardest object computed in this work; the agreement is nonetheless
sufficient to exhibit the qualitative structure of $Q$ on representative
slices (\cref{fig:eigenfunction}).
